A guide for school leaders

Year-end giving, in one afternoon

Asking for money can feel awkward, especially from families you already ask for tuition. A clear ask with real numbers gives people a way to help. This plan takes one afternoon: three emails and a thank-you, sent between December 1 and 31.

See the plan Get the AI prompt

Your afternoon, step by step

About two hours, any day before December 1.

  1. Check how your school can take gifts10 min
  2. Set up one link and three amounts20 min
  3. Build your list30 min
  4. Write your emails with AI30 min
  5. Load them into Gmail15 min
  6. Thank every donor within 48 hoursEach day

Then send three emails, each on a Tuesday

  • Dec 1The letter
  • Dec 15One story
  • Dec 29Last call

Send each email only to people who have not given yet. Anyone who gives gets a thank-you instead.

Starting after December 1? Follow the same steps, send your letter as soon as it is ready, and keep the December 29 last call. A story email fits in between if you have a week to spare.

Step 1

Check how your school can take gifts

Whether a gift is tax-deductible depends on how your school is set up. Settle it before you write a word.

  • A 501(c)(3) nonprofitGifts are usually deductible. Use this guide as written.
  • Part of a church or another nonprofitGifts often go through that body. Ask who issues the receipt and whose name goes on the letter.
  • For-profit, including most microschools and LLCsGifts are not deductible. Ask anyway and say so plainly, or work with a fiscal sponsor: a nonprofit that takes gifts for a program it oversees.
  • In a state with tax-credit scholarshipsA donor may earn a state credit by giving to the scholarship group that serves your families. That gift goes to the group, not to you.

Never call a gift deductible unless you know it is. This is general information, not tax advice: check with your accountant.

Step 2

Set up one link and three amounts

One giving link that opens on a phone, shows your three amounts and asks for no account. If the tool you use for tuition payments offers a donation page, start there.

Most donation tools are free to start and make their money from card fees or from a tip they ask your donor to add. Before you choose one, check what you pay on each gift and whether donors see a tip request.

Price each amount from one number: what it costs to teach one student for one school day.

Yearly spending ÷ students ÷ school days (often about 180)

Example at $42.50 a school day. Round yours to clean numbers.

Name what gifts will fund

  • Scholarships
  • Supplies
  • Field trips
  • Gas cards for families
  • Operating costs
  • Special programs

Tie each amount to one of them, priced from your own costs: a day of one student’s learning, a field trip seat, a month of gas for one family.

A gift given for a named use must be spent on it. To keep room to choose, ask for gifts “where they are needed most.” Your accountant can show you how to track named gifts.

Step 3

Build your list

Start with people who already know your school. Never buy a list: strangers ignore it, and it can send your school’s mail to spam.

  • Previous donorsPast receipts and your payment tool. Ask them first: they are the likeliest to give again.
  • Current familiesEnrollment forms and your school email list. Ask each family to forward the letter to two relatives.
  • Friends and familyYour phone contacts, and your staff’s and board’s. Ask each board member for ten names.
  • VendorsYour accounting system’s vendor list. Ask for a sponsorship or donated goods.
  • Your landlordAsk in person for a gift, a sponsorship, or what only they can give: a repair, an upgrade, a month of lower rent.
  • Former familiesOld enrollment lists. Tell them what has changed since they left.
  • Your boardAsk every member to give before December 1. Some funders ask what share of your board gives, and “all of them” is the best answer.

Let AI combine your lists

Export each list (look for “Export to CSV”) and paste them all into your own AI assistant with this prompt. Take out anything about students first. 

I am building one email list for my school’s year-end giving. Below are several lists I exported. Combine them into one table with these columns, in this order: Email, First name, Last name, Group, Gave in 2026.

Rules:
- One row per email address. If an address appears twice, keep one row.
- Group is one of: Previous donor, Current family, Friends and family, Vendor, Landlord, Former family, Board, Partner. If a person fits two, use the one that comes first in that list.
- Leave Gave in 2026 blank.
- Never invent or guess an email address or a name. Leave out any row with no email, and list those names separately at the end so I can find their addresses.
- Return the table as CSV I can paste into Google Sheets.

My lists:
[paste your lists here]

AI will not find email addresses for you, and one that tries will guess. Paste the result into the first tab of a Google Sheet and read it over once.

Step 4

Write your emails with AI

Donors give to results they can picture. Build the letter on four numbers:

  1. One number that changed this year. Students served, seats added, families waiting.
  2. What a gift buys, in your own costs. A day, a week or a month of one student’s learning.
  3. What comes next, and what it costs. The next classroom, grade or hire.
  4. One ask, with a date. One amount, one deadline, one link.

Find your story

Numbers show what a gift does. A story makes people care. Pick one student, one family or one teacher, ask their permission first (a family’s, for a student), use a first name only, and tell it in three short parts:

  1. Before. Where they were at the start of the year.
  2. What changed. What your school did, in one plain sentence.
  3. What a gift makes possible next. The same help for the next family.

Last spring, Maya’s family moved across town and lost their ride to school. A $50 gas card each month got her back to class, and she finished the year reading on grade level. Four more families need the same help this winter.

A made-up example. Tell your own, with permission.

Fill in the brackets, paste this into your AI assistant, and check every number before you send.

You are helping me write year-end giving emails for my small school. Write the four emails below now, with no questions first.

About my school:
- Name: [school name]
- Students this year: [number]. Last year: [number].
- Families waiting for a seat: [number, or leave out]
- Cost to teach one student for one school day: $[amount]
- My three gift amounts: $[day], $[week], $[month]
- What gifts will fund: [scholarships / supplies / field trips / gas cards / operating costs / a special program, or "where needed most"]
- What we want to do next, and what it costs: [next step and cost]
- One true story, first name only, with the family's permission: [before / what changed / what a gift makes possible next]
- Giving link: [link]
- Gifts to us are tax-deductible: [yes / no / not sure]
- My name and role: [name, role]

Write:
1. A December 1 letter under 250 words built on four things: one number that changed this year, what a gift buys in our own costs, what comes next and what it costs, and one ask with a December 31 deadline. Open with one sentence of the story.
2. A December 15 email of five sentences or fewer that tells the story in full.
3. A December 29 email of two lines: our year-end giving closes Thursday, and the link.
4. A thank-you I can send within 48 hours.

Rules: short sentences that read well on a phone. A subject line for each. Tell readers what their gift does; never describe our students by what they lack. Use only the numbers above, and leave a [blank] for anything I did not give you. If gifts are not tax-deductible or I am not sure, say nothing about deductions.
See an example letter

This fall we opened our doors to 48 students, up from 31 last year, and 12 more families are waiting for a seat.

It costs us $850 to teach one student for a month. A gift of $85 covers about two school days.

Next fall we want to add a fourth-grade room. That takes $18,000 for a teacher’s first semester and the furniture to fill it.

Will you give $100 by December 31 to help us get there? You can give at [your link].

Made-up figures. Use your own.

Use only your own photos, and only of students whose families have said yes.

Step 5

Load them into Gmail

Gmail mail merge sends each person their own copy with their first name filled in. Nobody sees anyone else’s address, and replies come back to you one by one. It needs a paid Google Workspace plan: Business Standard or Plus, Workspace Individual, Enterprise, or Education Standard or Plus (Google’s guide).

  1. Keep your list on the first tab of the sheet, with the header row Email, First name, Last name.
  2. In Gmail, click Compose, then Use mail merge to the right of the To line, and turn it on.
  3. Click Add from a spreadsheet, pick your sheet, match the Email and First name columns, and click Finish.
  4. In the email, type @ where the name belongs and pick the first-name column.
  5. Test it: send from a copy of the sheet that holds only your own row.
  6. Save it as a draft, and send it on its day. Gmail allows 1,500 mail merge emails a day and adds an unsubscribe link to each.

Before December 15 and December 29, type “yes” under Gave in 2026 for each donor, then send from a copy of the sheet without those rows.

On free Gmail (an address ending in @gmail.com) there is no mail merge, and Gmail sends 500 emails a day. Use a mail merge add-on from the Google Workspace Marketplace (check what it does with your contacts first), or write to your top 25 one by one.

Step 6

Thank every donor within 48 hours

A quick thank-you, by name and from you, is what turns a first gift into a second.

Dear [name],

Thank you for your gift of $[amount] on [date]. It covers [what it buys], and it brings us closer to [your next step].

[Your school’s legal name] received this gift and gave no goods or services in return.

With thanks,
[Your name]

For a nonprofit school, that email is also the receipt. A donor needs a written acknowledgment to deduct any single gift of $250 or more: your school’s name, the amount, and whether they got anything in return (IRS). If someone pays more than $75 and gets something back, such as a dinner ticket, you owe them a separate written disclosure (IRS). Have your accountant check your wording once, then reuse it.

A for-profit school drops the receipt line and says nothing about deductions.

On January 2, write down four numbers

  • DonorsEveryone who gave
  • Total raisedEvery gift added up
  • Average giftTotal ÷ donors
  • Repeat donorsAlso gave last year

Next December starts from them. In the spring, send each donor one line on what their gift did.
